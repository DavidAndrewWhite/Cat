## Reference material

### Starships

- **Corporate frigate**: Vaguely resembles a trilobite. The "head" of the trilobite is a spherical stack of decks for the crew, with "up" in the direction of thrust. The front of the sphere is a cluster of sensors. The cylindrical middle lobe contains the reactors and drive systems, with things like ribbed glowing blue heat sinks along the length of the cylinder. The port and starboard lobes are thinner, and are there to launch fairly large torpedoes (FTL missiles) by jettisoning them in the pitch directions of the ship ("up" and "down" relative to the viewer). The outermost edges of the port and starboard lobes host a point defense grid.  

- **Mark IX corvette**: This is smaller than the **Corporate frigate**, but shares some broadly similar design notes, like the "up" direction of the decks relative to the direction of thrust. The vessel is built around a broad, squat cylinder. The cylinder is divided into six equally large modular sectors surrounding the skinnier cylinder housing the main drive and reactor. From a view of the front of the cylinder, the drive system's dome shaped heat sink glows pale blue -- Fe Arran tech tries to improve the efficiency of radiation cooling by using higher energy (bluer) photons to increase dissipation. Starting from the 12 to 2 o'clock position on the main hull, the modules are a pressurized cargo/passenger bay with a panoramic window on the outside of the cylinder, then (2-4) a weapons module with a squat spinal-mounted energy weapon towards the middle and four small FTL missile launchers on the outside, then (4-6) a non-pressurized cargo module with some external loading ports for provisions and fuel, then (6-8) a less modular section that houses engineering, lab space, and the hinge point of the lower boom, then (8-10) base of the crew hull (which will be described in a moment), and then (10-12) crew quarters and the hinge point of the upper boom. The booms are abound three times as long as the diameter of the ship and carry slightly smaller versions of the drives in the main hull on the ends, with red thrust pits on the ends and an elongated blue heat sink on the outside. The booms can be moved through a 180° arc for off center-of-gravity thrust in order to quickly re-orient the vessel, and are currently splayed surrounding the crew hull. The visible "top" part of the crew hull is a canopy-like superstructure bearing a prominent sensor array built over the "bottom" of the crew hull, which resembles the prow of a naval vessel. This protrudes outwards about one half the diameter of the main hull, and is where the bridge and staterooms are. The center of gravity should be between the crew and main hulls. The opposite end of the crew hull is slightly flattened, and contains a small fork-mounted turret with a miniature energy weapon as a kind of "deck gun". The one in this story has been partially demilitarized, and the modular construction means that individual Mark IX's can vary -- a military load out might have two weapons modules, or a specialized module to carry a more useful load of torpedoes (FTL missiles), and a military vessel is unlikely to have a picture window.  

*(Now, for some ships that are very much **not** following the above design norms.)*

- **The Nightstalker**: The "Star Trek" inspiration of some of the elements of Corporate military vessels was the set-up for a joke, and the "Nightstalker" is the punch line. This is the *hero ship* for *Honor Guard*, who are *kayfabe* space cops for "Fun Division". The ship resembles a garden spade. The main hull is a rounded triangle, like the shovel part of the spade. A bubble containing the bridge are on top of the gently domed superstructure above the "shovel". A strut protrudes from the middle rear of the main hull, and a single nacelle holds the main drive -- this is the "handle" of the spade. A final touch is a visual swipe from Star Trek's "U.S.S. Defiant", a sort of roll bar over the bridge bump on which comically over-sized dome lights are mounted, like a police car. The deck orientation is *not* in the direction of thrust, but in the direction of the nacelle -- while the inertial compensators do their best, the crew end up having to lean on hard maneuvers. There are some advantages to this design, though. This set-up is common for luxury civilian spacecraft, especially yachts and pleasure craft that do a lot of high-speed atmospheric flight, and makes it easier to produce the *Honor Guard* series.  

- **Bastard Sun**: The name is a corruption of "Bas Tersnn", the name originally given by the Reyll military. Translated, it's "Liberated/Detached Ship"; it's not really a proper ship name, a vessel that's not part of an active fleet would be called this. It was been heavily modified, and incorporates a lot of Corporate technology, but the hull and most of the systems are original Reyll manufacture. There are three main hulls that are squat cylinders, rounded in front, flat in back. Round tubes and several braces in a circle connect the hulls, leaving the center open. Each hull has a large turret with a squat energy weapon a little behind the middle of the hull. An elongated, curved, blade-like structure runs through the braces at the midpoints between the hulls. "Top" for the decks corresponds to the direction of thrust -- usually. The Reyll get "creative" with simulated gravity, and are prone to using the tech to do chairs in software, or elevators operated by flinging yourself into what looks like an open shaft. While one hull has the crew, the other two hulls contain a reactor each; a goal is redundancy and tolerance to severe battle damage. The engineering hulls have gravity where "down" towards the outside of the cylinder, and "up" is towards the center line -- equipment is on motorized racks that can be pulled down from the ceiling.  

### Computer interfaces and retro-tech

- **Corporate technology** can seem less advanced than it should be. The Fe Arrans have sophisticated neural interfaces, very advanced autonomous computers for control systems, and extensive cultural familiarity with altered states of consciousness. I'll be happy to admit that some of this is arguably lazy storytelling, to help real-life human readers fill in gaps with familiar elements. Some of it, however, has an in-universe rationale. The designers of the Corporate frigate have either seen "Star Trek", or have been provided designs directly or indirectly inspired by the 1960's TV show. The control panel layouts are intended to provide visual and haptic feedback, and are more flexible than they might first appear. Rarely used and potentially dangerous functions are accessed using keyboards and screens in order to discourage casual reconfiguration of critical systems. The fire team Lancer's "binoculars" are not dramatically better than his powered armor's senor suite, but they do serve as a visual indicator for the rest of the fire team that their squad leader is picking out a target. The Corporation pretends to care about efficiency, and are committed to their pretenses. The actual capabilities of their technology will shine through now and again.  

- **Reyll technology** is less bound by these conventions and more "alien" feeling. It is quite straightforward for a neural interface to alter sensory perceptions, adding data from other systems. Neural interfaces can also open entirely new sensory channels -- it is quite possible to effectively grow a new eye, with a perceptually distinct stream of visual information, which is fairly easy for most people to handle with minimal orientation and training. Adding new sensory pathways or extending existing ones, e.g. by adding a new primary color to a user's visual color space is quite possible using Fe Arran tech. This is probably going to pose challenges for visual storytelling. I hope these are interesting challenges. 

- **"Artificial Gravity" and living areas in spaceships**:  
  - I wanted to avoid some of the unfortunate implications of "artificial gravity" tropes. The problem with taking "simulated gravity" literally is that gravity is a force with infinite range that messes with metrics. The most plausible physical effect that could possibly replace it is the centrifugal pseudo-force, which has some serious weirdness with Coriolis forces and other manifestations of conserved angular momentum, e.g. gradients between passenger heads and feet. The Fe Arran solution are plates mounted in the ceiling and floor which use tractor/pressor beams. Also, gravity is a lot like air pressure -- you only need a little to avoid bad effects, adapting to lower gravity is easy, and you have to deal with fewer injured crew members from tripping and falling.  
  - While I compare the **Mark IX corvette** to a submarine, keep in mind that it is designed for Fe Arrans, who are aggressive and territorial obligate carnivores with fur and dander. The ventilation systems are amazing -- you will never need to dust on a Fe Arran ship. Smoking is not a problem on anything bigger than a fighter or an escape pod. Quarters will have at most four bunks; more bunks in a room will lead to fistfights unless the crew in question are clones specially conditioned to put up with closer quarters or are all in some kind of polycule.  
  - **Warning:** most of this discussion is about military and industrial ships. Things get *weird* with luxury yachts and vessels like the **Honor Guard** ship *Nightstalker*.  

### Fantasy and Science Fiction

- **Consistent inconsistency** is another potential problem to navigate. Magic coexists with technology coexists with mystical visions and psychology. This setting is very much a *science fantasy* world with superheroes, space wizards, reactionless drives, and teleportation. Technology should feel familiar but grounded; it should subjectively feel superficially plausible. To the extent technology overlaps with magic, like how the star ships don't need reaction mass for propulsion, or how FTL communication and sensors are possible, the technology should be presented as a "black box" and familiar genre conventions should be leveraged where possible. Magic should be presented as a way to bend or break the rules of strict "realism". There may be some use cases where the clash between these approaches should be a source of humor, e.g. a ship's mage sacrificing a small animal to fix a recalcitrant FTL drive, or that orbit-to-surface teleportation is done by space wizards with some technological assistance to help them survive handling the conservation of momentum issues. Standard artifacts of the science fiction genre that blatantly violate physics *are* magic. Similarly, magic *is* a manifestation of arguably *real* cultural and psychological phenomena. I'm hoping some humor is to be had from pointing out how goofy a lot of pop science fiction really is. The space ships look like Temu "Star Trek", fly like the ships from "Babylon 5", "The Expanse", or the 21st century "Battlestar Galactica", and have magical bullshit that keeps the crew's necks from snapping as they violently rotate around their center of gravity. Said magical bullshit may be exploited as a source of sensible chuckles.  

- **Magic** should feel familiar to anyone who has ever played a fantasy RPG. While references can and will be made to esoteric practices like yoga or the martial arts in depicting it, casting a spell will usually include the utterance of a *word of power*. Depending on the context, this *word of power* may or may not be intelligible; if it is intelligible, it should provide an elliptical hint to what is about to happen. Lettering for a *word of power* should be easily distinguishable from ordinary dialogue or narration.

### Miscellaneous notes

- **Religion and the esoteric** are also going to be mined for their inherent absurdity. We live in a world where people sincerely believe that if an individual doesn't explain their sexual misdeeds in explicit detail to the anthropomorphic creator of the physical universe every day then that individual lacks any ability to distinguish right from wrong. The problem with mining this vein of absurdity is that religious groups do routinely suffer from discrimination. The point of the exercise isn't to persecute any religious or esoteric group, it's to have a cheap laugh at the absurdities implicit in a superficial reading of their practices.  

- **Politics** is another source for absurdity, although the mechanics are going to be a little different. As a concrete example, "American exceptionalism" is fairly mainstream, but that Theosophist groups will take this to the point that they believe America's "Founding Fathers" are Ascended Masters and members of the Great White Brotherhood? Pointing out the similarities between the "weird" and "normal" versions of these ideologies should be fair game. Pointing out that the Nazis' "Arier" have more to do with Helene Blavatsky and esoteric teachings than history, linguistics, or archaeology is also on the menu.
